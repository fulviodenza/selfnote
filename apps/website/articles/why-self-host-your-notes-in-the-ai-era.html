Why self-host your notes in the AI era

Your notes went from a text box to a training set. Here is why that makes running your own server the practical choice, not the austere one.

For twenty years the argument for hosting your own software was about control in the abstract: your data, your machine, your rules. It convinced the people it was always going to convince. Then the AI era arrived and the argument stopped being abstract. Your notes are now the most valuable thing you can hand to a model, and every notes vendor knows it.

Notes used to be a text box. Now they are a training set.

A notes app holds the most honest record of a person that exists anywhere: what you are working on, what you are afraid of, who you met, what you decided and why. Until recently that text sat in a database and was mostly read by you. The economics of a hosted notes app were storage and sync, both cheap, so the product could afford to leave your data alone.

Language models changed what that text is worth. Your notes are perfect grounding material: personal, long-lived, written in your own voice. Every hosted vendor is now shipping "AI features", and every one of those features needs your notes sent to a model. Read the terms of service updates of the last two years with that in mind. The words to look for are "improve our services" and "trusted partners".

Three questions a hosted vendor cannot answer well

  1. Which model saw my notes, and where does it run? With a hosted app, the answer is whichever provider they signed with this quarter, in whichever region was cheapest. You are not told when it changes.
  2. Can I turn it off? Often you can turn off the button. You can rarely turn off the indexing that makes the button work, and the indexing is where the text leaves.
  3. What happens when the company pivots? Notes apps get acquired, sunset, and "reimagined" at a rate that would be funny if the content were not your life. Export tools exist, but the export is never the whole thing.

Self-hosting answers all three in one move

When the server is yours, the AI question becomes a configuration choice instead of a trust exercise. Selfnote ships with AI off. You turn it on by giving your server one provider, and the choice is yours: a Claude command line tool running on the server itself, so the text never leaves the box; the Anthropic API, if you are happy with that trade; or an Ollama model on a GPU in your closet, which is the first time "my notes never leave the house" has been true of an AI feature. The three options are compared here.

Turning it off is deleting one environment variable. There is no indexing you cannot see, because the whole server is open source and the AI code is one file.

And the pivot risk inverts. A vendor pivot takes your notes with it. A self-hosted project that stops being maintained leaves you with a Postgres database you already own, a documented schema, and clients that keep working against the last version forever.

The part people get wrong: self-hosting is not giving up on AI

The old framing was privacy versus convenience, and self-hosting sat on the privacy end looking austere. That is backwards now. The most useful AI features are the ones with the deepest access, and deep access is exactly what you cannot responsibly grant to a vendor's model. On your own server you can.

Selfnote's MCP server is what that looks like. Any Claude, in a conversation about anything, can search your notes, read one, propose an edit to it, file a summary of the chat under the right page, or ask what is overdue this week. That is a level of integration no hosted vendor will give a third-party model, because they want their own model to be the one you talk to. On your server there is no such conflict of interest. See what Claude can do with your notes.

What it costs

Less than it did. A Selfnote server is three small processes and a Postgres; it runs on the machine that already hosts your other services, or on a five dollar VPS. The Helm chart does backups and point in time recovery through CloudNativePG. The clients are free, on web, desktop, iPhone, iPad and Android, and they work offline. The self-hosting guide is an evening's work for someone who has run a container before.

The price of the alternative is harder to see but not smaller: your notes, read by a model you did not pick, under terms that will change, to build a product you do not own.
