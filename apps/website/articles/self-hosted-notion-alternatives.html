The best self-hosted Notion alternatives in 2026

AppFlowy, AFFiNE, Docmost, Outline, Anytype, SiYuan, Trilium, Joplin and Selfnote, compared honestly: licence, shape, collaboration, offline and AI, and which one fits which reason for leaving.

People leave Notion for one of a few reasons: the notes are on someone else's servers, the per-seat price grows with the team, it needs to be online, or the AI features send everything to a model nobody chose. Every tool below answers at least one of those. None of them is Notion, and the best one for you depends on which reason brought you here. Selfnote is on the list, and it is not the right answer for everyone.

Licences matter when "open source" is the reason you are switching, and the server is where the exceptions hide. Outline is source available under the Business Source License, and Anytype under its own source available licence; both let you self-host, but neither is open source in the OSI sense. AppFlowy's apps are AGPL but the server you would self-host now lives in a separate repository under a commercial licence with a free tier of one seat. Joplin's apps are AGPL but Joplin Server is under a personal-use licence. AFFiNE, Docmost, SiYuan, Trilium and Selfnote are open source end to end.

AppFlowy

The closest thing to Notion you can run yourself. Pages, nested workspaces, and databases with grid, board and calendar views, in desktop and mobile apps that work offline and sync through AppFlowy's cloud or a server of your own. It has AI features and can use local models. If what you miss from Notion is the database views, start here. Two things to know before you do: the self-host server has moved to a separate, commercially licensed repository with a free tier of one seat, so check the terms against your use, and running it is more involved than a single container.

AFFiNE

Docs plus an infinite whiteboard in one workspace, local-first, with databases inside pages. The whiteboard is the reason to pick it: brainstorming, diagrams and notes on one canvas. Self-hosting is a Docker compose, and the AI copilot stays off until you give it a provider key of your own. It is MIT licensed, which is the most permissive licence on this list.

Docmost

A team wiki in the Confluence shape: spaces, pages, real-time editing, comments, permissions. The fastest route from a fresh server to a working team workspace on this list, and a good choice if a handbook is the job. Not aimed at personal notes, no offline mode, and the AI features need the enterprise licence.

Outline

A polished team knowledge base with real-time editing, collections and a strong search. It expects an external identity provider for sign-in, so plan on configuring one. The licence is BSL 1.1: fine for self-hosting, not open source.

Anytype

A personal knowledge base built on objects and relations, local-first, peer to peer, end to end encrypted, with apps on every platform. Collaboration happens between your own devices and shared spaces rather than through a central server, and you can run your own sync node. The model takes learning, and the licence is source available.

SiYuan

A local-first block editor with a strong outliner and database-like attributes, with desktop and mobile apps. Sync is a paid feature, through its own cloud or your own S3 or WebDAV storage, and the AI writing features use your own OpenAI key. No real-time collaboration; it is a personal tool first.

Trilium

Hierarchical notes with a self-hosted sync server, scripting, and a long history. The desktop app is the main client and the web client covers the rest. Built for one person with a large tree of notes rather than for a team.

Joplin

Markdown notebooks with apps everywhere, end to end encryption, and a Joplin Server you can self-host for sync, under a personal-use licence rather than the apps' AGPL. Version 3.7 added a beta AI chat, semantic search and an MCP server, with your own provider. No real-time collaboration and no block editor; if you want plain Markdown files and a stable, boring tool, it is hard to beat.

Selfnote

Pages in a tree with a block editor, where every page is a CRDT: real-time collaboration with cursors that keeps working when one person is offline and merges cleanly after. Tasks with due dates and a calendar feed, links and backlinks with a graph, version history, share links with view counts, and an Obsidian vault importer. AI is off by default; you enable one provider, a Claude command line tool on your server, the Anthropic API, or Ollama. In-app actions give you a draft to insert, and edits proposed from outside the editor arrive as a diff you accept. An MCP server lets Claude search and write to your notes from any conversation.

What it lacks: Notion-style databases and views, a template gallery, and a marketplace. The iPhone and desktop apps are built from source until they ship as installers; Android and web are ready. Two Rust services, a static web client and a Postgres, with a Helm chart that includes backups. The honest comparison with Notion is on the Notion alternative page.

Not quite self-hosted

Obsidian and Logseq come up in every search for a Notion alternative. Both keep notes as local files, which is a fine answer to the ownership question. Obsidian is not open source and its sync is a paid service, or a community plugin with your own storage. Logseq is open source; its sync is paid or in beta, and pointing the app at a sync server of your own is new and not the usual path. If local files are enough and you do not need collaboration, they are excellent. If you want a server you own that several people and devices share, the tools above are the list.

Which one

  • You miss Notion's databases: AppFlowy, then AFFiNE.
  • You need a team wiki: Docmost, or Outline if you already run an identity provider.
  • You want a whiteboard next to your notes: AFFiNE.
  • Strict privacy and encryption for one person: Anytype or Joplin.
  • A large personal tree of notes: Trilium or SiYuan.
  • Real-time collaboration that survives offline, tasks, and AI on your own terms: Selfnote.

Whichever you pick, do the export test on day one: take your notes out, look at what you got, and make sure you could leave. That is the whole point of leaving Notion.
