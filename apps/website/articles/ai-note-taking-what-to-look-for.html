What to look for in an AI note taking app

Every notes app has an AI tab now and the feature lists look identical. Eight questions that separate them, and a checklist of good answers.

Every notes app now has an AI tab. The feature lists look identical: summarise, rewrite, ask questions about your notes. The differences that will matter to you in two years are not on the feature list. Here is what to check before you move your notes into anything with "AI" in the pitch.

1. Where does the text go when you press the button?

This is the only question that matters for privacy, and most products answer it vaguely. Ask: which model, run by whom, in which region, and does the vendor keep a copy? A good answer names the provider and lets you change it. The best answer lets you run the model yourself. Selfnote supports three providers, two of which keep the text on hardware you control.

2. Can the AI change your notes without you seeing the change?

A summary you paste is harmless. An assistant that edits pages in place is useful and dangerous in equal measure. Look for a review step: a diff, an accept button, an undo that is more than a toast. Selfnote's note actions produce a draft you insert yourself, edits arriving from outside the editor are staged as proposals you accept or reject, and version history means even an accepted mistake is recoverable. More on that here.

3. Is the AI a feature or an indexing pipeline?

"Ask your notes" features need the notes indexed, and indexing means the text has been processed and often stored by the provider before you ask anything. Check whether turning the feature off turns the pipeline off. In a self-hosted setup the answer is visible: Selfnote sends note text only when you invoke an action, and only the note or selection you invoked it on, plus any extra notes you explicitly add as context.

4. Does it work offline, and does it merge?

AI features are built on connectivity; note taking is not. The app must be fully usable on a plane, and edits made on two devices while disconnected must merge without a conflict dialog. This is a hard engineering problem that most apps solve with "last write wins", which is a polite name for losing your work. Selfnote's notes are CRDTs, which is the actual solution.

5. Can another AI get in?

The assistant you want to use in two years is not the one your notes vendor ships today. Ask whether the app exposes an open protocol, such as MCP, so the model you choose can read and write your notes under your rules. Vendors with their own model rarely do, because they would rather you talked to theirs. Selfnote's MCP server gives any Claude thirteen tools over your workspace, behind a token you can revoke.

6. What does the exit look like?

Export is a feature everyone has and nobody tests. Ask whether the export includes the structure, the links between notes, the attachments and the history, and whether the format is readable without the app. For a self-hosted app the exit is a database you already have a dump of. For a hosted one it is a zip file and a hope.

7. Who pays for the model, and how?

AI costs money per request, and a subscription notes app has to recover it. Watch for usage caps, "AI credits" and tiers. A self-hosted app lets you bring the plan you already pay for: Selfnote can run your Claude subscription on your server with no per request bill, or a local model for free.

8. Is it open source?

Not as a matter of ideology. As a matter of being able to check the answers to questions one to seven instead of taking them on faith. Selfnote's AI code is a single file, and the clients are in the same repository.

A short checklist

AskGood answer
Where does note text go?A named provider you can change, or your own hardware.
Can AI edit silently?No. Diff, accept, history.
Is there a background index?Only what you send, when you send it.
Offline?Fully, with real merges.
Other models?Open protocol, revocable token.
Exit?Your database, documented schema.
Who pays for AI?Bring your own plan or model.
Open source?Yes, including the server.

If a product answers all eight well, it is probably self-hosted, open source, and treats the model as a provider rather than the product. That is not a coincidence, and it is the shape Selfnote was built in. The self-hosting guide is where to start.
